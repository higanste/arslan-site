Spotting fake emails and scams (they target small shops too)

Quick answer

"Phishing" means a fake message pretending to be someone you trust, trying to steal a password or a payment. The tell-tale signs: urgency, weird sender addresses, and links that don't match where they claim to go. When unsure, don't click â€” go to the real website yourself.

"We're too small to be a target." That's what most owners think â€” and it's backwards. Big companies have IT departments. Small shops have one person doing the books at 10 p.m. Scammers know it, which is why fake invoices and "your account is suspended" emails land in small-business inboxes every day.

The three signs of a fake

One: urgency. "Pay this invoice today or your service stops." Real companies rarely need a decision in 20 minutes. Two: a mismatched sender. Hover over (or tap) the sender name â€” the real address may be something like service@randomletters.net while the email pretends to be your bank. Three: a link that doesn't go where it says. Press and hold (or hover) the link to preview the true address. If the message claims to be from Canada Post but the link leads to a strange website, that's your answer.

Spelling mistakes used to be the giveaway; modern scams write better than that now, so don't rely on it.

The scams that catch small businesses

The classics: a fake "overdue invoice" with an attachment, a "CTA/cra" message threatening penalties, a "courier couldn't deliver your package" click-here, and the sneakiest â€” an email that looks like it's from your boss or a regular supplier, asking to change the bank details for payment. That last one deserves a phone call: always confirm any bank-detail change by calling the number you already have, never one printed in the email.

Make your team a hard target

Ten minutes of training beats a year of worry. Tell your staff the rule: pause on anything about money, logins, or urgency, and ask you first.
